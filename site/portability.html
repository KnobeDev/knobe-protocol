Knowledge Should Outlive Its Platform

A manifesto for radical knowledge portability. By David Kyle. A non-normative companion to KNOBE Protocol v1.

The content travels. What made it knowledge often does not.

We can move a document across the planet in a second. We can copy it into a chat, feed it to a model, summarize it for a meeting, translate it for a colleague, and insert its conclusions into a report. We call this portability. Yet the very conditions that make the document worth reading may not make the trip.

The words arrive. The sources do not. The attribution becomes vague. The limitations disappear. The decisions behind an accessible adaptation are forgotten. A summary is mistaken for the source it compressed. An AI-generated synthesis begins to circulate as though it were an independently established finding. The content is mobile, but the knowledge has become less portable with every handoff.

This is not simply a problem of dishonesty or inadequate citation. It is an architectural habit. We have built increasingly powerful systems for storing, finding, connecting, governing, and transforming knowledge, while too often treating the document that leaves those systems as their final residue: the file left over when the real infrastructure stays behind.

The document should not always be the afterthought of the system.

Knowledge has always traveled under conditions

There was never a golden age in which meaning moved intact. Oral traditions, manuscripts, libraries, scholarly citations, archives, credentials, and bureaucratic records all developed ways of preserving selected context while compressing everything else. A citation stands in for a chain of intellectual debts. A diploma stands in for years of study. An archive's finding aid preserves relationships that an isolated document may not reveal. Institutions have long decided which compressed representations count, who may interpret them, and what authority they carry.

These devices are useful precisely because they leave things out. We cannot transport the entire history of an idea every time we use it. Compression makes knowledge possible at scale. But it also creates a recurring danger: what survives becomes mistaken for the whole. The record is taken for the event; the credential for competence; the summary for the investigation; the quotation for the speaker's full position.

AI makes this old problem newly consequential. A passage can pass through a retrieval system, a language model, an agent, a graph, a dashboard, and a second agent before anyone asks what was lost. Each transformation may be sensible on its own. The cumulative result may be a confident output detached from the conditions under which its sources were produced.

Everyone is now responsible for more transformations. That responsibility is not carried by individuals alone: developers, organizations, educators, publishers, and AI system operators also decide which context survives. But more people must learn to ask a new kind of question: What, exactly, did this transformation preserve, and what did it leave behind?

Portability is more than one property

A file can be portable in one sense and stranded in another.

It may be artifact-portable: anyone can open it. Yet it may not be interpretively portable: its terms, assumptions, and limitations are missing. Its content may remain readable while its evidentiary portability fails: the source and transformation history cannot be inspected. Its attribution may travel without independently established authority. Its use conditions may travel without enforcement. An agent may parse its text without understanding the restrictions or fidelity limits that accompanied it.

A student can carry a final essay from a university into a new job but lose the record of how it was researched, revised, challenged, and adapted. A researcher can move a transcript into another tool while leaving behind the consent terms that governed it. A person using a screen reader can receive a technically intact document whose essential diagram has become an unlabeled image. A creator can take the file and lose the context that gives it intellectual identity.

These are different portability failures. They occur at different boundaries: between formats, applications, agents, institutions, people, and generations. No single checklist of file extensions captures them.

And no legitimate portability framework can treat accessibility as a negotiable bonus. If a file survives transfer but its recipient cannot navigate or understand the knowledge through an accessible representation, the transfer has failed that recipient. An intact cryptographic hash is no consolation for an unreadable table.

Portability without accessibility is incomplete portability.

A test inside Microsoft 365

Consider an instructor preparing a learning module in Microsoft Word. She uses proper heading styles, meaningful alternative text, structured tables, and an accessible PDF export. The team collaborates in Teams, revises the document in SharePoint, and uses Copilot to prepare a summary. Microsoft 365 can provide substantial accessibility, version history, governance, and audit capabilities. These are real strengths, not shortcomings to dismiss.

Now the instructor moves to another institution. She exports the document for a colleague who has no access to the original Microsoft 365 tenant. The DOCX or tagged PDF may remain fully usable and accessible. But the recipient does not automatically receive the SharePoint version history, Teams discussions, selected Copilot interactions, or the institutional context of every decision. Some of those records should not travel: they may be confidential, ephemeral, or subject to policy. Others matter for responsible reuse, especially if the colleague is asked to revise the module or an AI agent is asked to summarize it again.

Imagine an additional, portable record accompanying the authorized export. It identifies the source, credits the contributors and accessibility adapter, declares which transformations were made, states what the plain-language version preserves and omits, and records selected conditions of reuse. The recipient can inspect that context without joining the original tenant. A later agent can consult it before making another derivative. The accessible Word or PDF representation remains available; the portable record does not replace it.

This is not a reason to abandon Microsoft 365. It is a reason to design for the moment when knowledge leaves any system, including an excellent one.

Reverse the dependency, without abolishing the platform

The prevailing pattern places the application at the center. The application stores the history, the database stores the relationships, the institution stores the authority, the agent assembles the context, and the document appears at the end. It is an output of the system, often thinner than the system that produced it.

We should also be able to build in the other direction.

Start with a durable knowledge object carrying selected context. Let an application edit it, a graph connect it, an agent interpret it, an institution evaluate it, and a collaborator transform it. When a consequential transformation creates a new object, let that object declare what it came from, what changed, and what it can responsibly claim. Let the result move on without needing permanent access to every environment it passed through.

This is an inversion of dependency, not a fantasy of complete independence. A file cannot replace a university registrar, a consent process, a live permission system, a rich knowledge graph, or a preservation archive. Nor should it contain everyone's private conversations or every trace of an agent's activity. Some facts must remain with authoritative systems. Some should not be disclosed at all.

The principle is narrower and stronger: context should not belong exclusively to the platform that assembled it.

A knowledge object should be able to carry the selected history and obligations needed for its next responsible use. The system can remain powerful without becoming the object's permanent custodian.

KNOBE is one implementation, not the entire idea

KNOBE Protocol v1 puts this principle into a deliberately small artifact. A .knobe.md file is ordinary readable Markdown accompanied by a machine-readable, hash-sealed declaration of selected context. It can record sources, transformations, fidelity limits, use conditions, and accessibility adaptation lineage. Its payload can be checked locally without an account or central registry. It can be read without specialized software, even when its receiving environment does not understand the protocol.

That is a modest technical choice with an ambitious consequence: a knowledge object can arrive carrying more of its own interpretive record instead of depending entirely on the application that last touched it.

But the limits matter. KNOBE's seal proves the integrity of the declared payload, not that the declaration is true. Authorship and identity are not independently proven by v1. The Markdown body's integrity is checked only when an optional body hash is present. A copied excerpt does not inherit the original file's seal. A use condition is a notice, not enforcement. An accessibility adaptation record is not an accessibility certification. No protocol can force a recipient to read what the object carries.

These limits are not excuses to retreat from portability. They define the work that remains. Verifiable Credentials and C2PA can add identity-backed attestations in suitable settings. W3C PROV and knowledge graphs can represent richer relationships. RO-Crate and BagIt can package collections and companion files. MCP and agent protocols can help tools exchange and act on context. Accessibility standards and human testing can establish whether people can actually use the resulting representations. The right architecture composes these capabilities instead of pretending one format should replace them all.

From context engineering to process literacy

Context engineering asks how systems obtain and use the information needed to act. Process literacy asks whether people can understand, evaluate, and take responsibility for the transformations that produced what they receive. Portability joins them. It asks whether the record of that work can cross the boundary between one encounter and the next.

For learners, this can mean carrying a meaningful intellectual history beyond a course platform. For researchers, it can mean preserving consent notices and the limits of a translation alongside the text. For creators, it can mean retaining the labor of revision and adaptation. For organizations, it can mean allowing context to move among approved tools without reconstructing it from scratch. For AI agents, it can mean encountering a source's declared limitations before treating its contents as authority.

This must not become a new regime of compulsory self-surveillance. Process records should be proportionate, privacy-preserving, and useful to the people who make them. A student's disability status is not a provenance field. A worker's every keystroke is not a necessary history of knowledge. The right to carry meaningful context must be accompanied by the ability to withhold what should remain private.

The future should not require permanent residence in someone else's system

The most consequential question about an intelligent infrastructure may not be how much it can remember while we are inside it. It may be what remains ours, and what remains intelligible, when we leave.

An archive, a university, a company, a cloud platform, and an AI agent may each make knowledge more useful. None should be presumed to be the only environment in which that knowledge can retain meaning. Nor should the recipient be forced to choose between rich context and independence, or between portability and accessibility.

We need systems that make knowledge more capable while letting knowledge outlive the systems. We need open objects that can be inspected, challenged, adapted, and carried forward. We need standards that compose, institutions that recognize evidence without monopolizing it, and tools that treat accessible transformations as intellectual work rather than afterthoughts.

The ambition is not a world without platforms. It is a world in which platforms enrich knowledge without owning its future.

Knowledge should outlive its platform. And the document should no longer have to be the afterthought.
